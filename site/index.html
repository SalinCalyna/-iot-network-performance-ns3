Analysis of Gateway-Centric IoT Wireless Mesh Networks Using NS-3

Two simulation studies of a multi-hop IoT mesh in which every sensor reports to one gateway. The frozen IEEE 802.11b study locates where packets are lost as the network grows, and tests traffic-rate reduction and an adaptive (sigmoid) routing metric. The Wi-Fi 6 study adds high-risk relay nodes to an 802.11ax mesh and measures how much risk-aware routing reduces exposure and loss.

1 · V3 Baseline — IEEE 802.11b

Network performance vs network size and traffic

1,782 runs: 3 protocols × 6 node counts × 3 traffic levels × 3 mobility modes (162 conditions) × 11 seeds (20–30), 300 s each. Values are pooled over traffic and mobility unless stated.

Key FindingPerformance becomes increasingly constrained as network scale and traffic load increase, with bottleneck effects becoming more pronounced at larger scales — a structural effect tied to topology and channel capacity, not a single protocol's failure.

2 · Bottleneck Analysis & Traffic-Rate Intervention — IEEE 802.11b

Where packets are lost, and whether less traffic helps

Per-node instrumentation (176 runs: OLSR and Static, N = 30/50/75/100, medium and high traffic, 11 seeds), retry-exhaustion validation (36 runs), offered-rate sweep (72 runs) and a Static N = 50 negative control (12 runs).

  • The PDR gain is monotonic across every tested seed. At N=100, delivered throughput rises at a reduced offered rate (12 kbps: +24.3% in seed-mean throughput; +25.5% as the mean of the three per-seed changes) — the signature of recovering from congestion collapse, not simply sending less.

Congestion was genuinely relieved, but the relieved mechanisms were never the dominant source of this condition's loss; most of it remains unattributed (64.65%–99.57%, typically ≥92%) — static_n50_diagnostic.csv.
  • Intervention effectiveness is protocol- and load-dependent and should not be overgeneralized to every condition.
3 · V4 Sigmoid Routing — IEEE 802.11b · valid negative result

Adaptive sigmoid routing changed routes, not performance

957 runs: 594 matched-condition runs over the full V3 condition grid, a 297-run parameter-sensitivity sweep and a 66-run Tier-1 activation screen (N = 50, medium traffic, 11 seeds per configuration).

Key FindingRouting activation ≠ performance improvement — the strongest-activation V4 configuration re-routes 100% of seeds but does not produce a statistically credible PDR gain.

The strongest-activation configuration (100% of seeds re-routed, mean hop count +27.9%) produced a PDR change of only +3.5%, not statistically credible (sign-test p=1.0), while delay worsened 21.6% and maximum link utilisation 42.8%. The mechanism works; it does not improve delivery under the tested conditions. Structural reason: the risk score reflects node degree (topology), whereas load concentrates on the gateway's one-hop neighbourhood regardless of degree. The two-pass offline design also measures load under baseline routing, never under the mechanism's own routing. The Wi-Fi 6 result below uses a different mechanism and is not a V4 result.

Wi-Fi 6 Risk-Aware Routing

Does routing around high-risk relay nodes reduce risk exposure and packet loss in a gateway-centric 802.11ax mesh, and what does it cost? Each sensor is LOW_RISK or HIGH_RISK; a high-risk relay loses each transit data packet with probability 0.5. Static (BFS shortest path), Risk-Aware (risk-weighted Dijkstra), OLSR and AODV are compared with k = 0–3 high-risk relays.

4 · Wi-Fi 6 — Secondary finding

AODV at N = 100

Measured—
  • Measured in this ns-3 configuration only (802.11ax, synchronized 8 kbps CBR, 100 sensors). AODV's PDR is nearly the same at every k, so the risk model is not the cause; at N = 30 (k = 0, 10 seeds) AODV delivers 99.99 %.
  • Confirmed in the ns-3 source: AODV treats every dropped unicast MPDU, including queue-lifetime expiry, as a link failure.
  • Hypothesis, not a demonstrated cause: a route-maintenance feedback loop (queue expiry → link failure → RERR and rediscovery → more airtime). The causal chain was not isolated, the Block Ack check covers one seed, and the 500 ms MAC queue lifetime was not varied. This is not a claim about AODV in general.
  • AODV results are reported as measured: no AODV parameter was tuned and no run was replaced. Risk-Aware's N = 100 comparison should be read against Static (matched) and OLSR.
  • Diagnostic tables and figures: T-W6a–c · F-W5–F-W7.

    Why protocol-independent placement changes the interpretation

    Subtree placement vs uniform placement (same seeds, k = 3)

    Each cell: subtree placement → uniform placement, mean over the same topology seeds 20–22. Descriptive.

      Statistical limitations

      • Nested draws. The 12 draws come from 3 topologies (4 placements each), so they are not fully independent. Inference covers random placement within these 3 topologies. The topology-level interval (n = 3) is shown for every comparison; with 3 topologies, no result is established for other topologies.
      • Risk-Aware vs Static cannot reach significance in this design. The two are identical in 7 of 12 draws, which leaves 5 non-zero differences. The smallest attainable sign-flip p is then 0.0625.
      • Precision. The Static − OLSR exposure interval spans about ±1.8 pp. It rules out a gap like the 37 pp seen under subtree placement, but it cannot confirm or reject the pre-registered prediction of |Δ| ≤ 1 pp.
      • Survivorship. Delay and hop metrics cover delivered packets only.
      • Same model assumptions as the main study: oracle risk knowledge for Risk-Aware, transit-only drops with probability 0.5, gateway never risky, one Wi-Fi 6 configuration (802.11ax, HE MCS0, synchronized CBR).

      How routes and route costs are calculated

      Static (BFS shortest-hop tree)

      Two sensors are linked when they are at most 90 m apart (--txRange). A breadth-first search from the gateway gives every sensor its hop count and a parent. Each sensor gets one host route, "gateway via parent", installed before traffic starts and never changed. When several neighbours are equally close, the first one found wins: neighbours are visited in node-id order.

      scratch/iot-network-wifi6-risk-v2.cc lines 910–957.

      Risk-Aware (risk-weighted Dijkstra)

      It uses the same 90 m link model. A route's cost is the sum, over every node the packet enters after leaving the source, of 1 + β·τv, where τv = −ln(1 − rv), rv is the node's risk score and τ = 0 at the gateway. With β = 10 and r = 0.5 for a high-risk relay, entering it costs 1 + 10·ln 2 ≈ 7.93, against 1 for a low-risk node. So a route through one risky relay loses to any safe route at most 6 hops longer. Ties are broken so that with no risk the tree equals Static's exactly.

      scratch/iot-network-wifi6-risk-v2.cc lines 1016–1056. Costs shown when you click a sensor are recomputed from this formula, and the build checks that they equal routes_planned.csv for every source in every run.

      How the Wi-Fi 6 study works · Architecture & simulation workflow

      From parameters to the published result

      The Wi-Fi 6 risk study is one ns-3 program run once per configuration, followed by read-only validation, analysis and publication steps. Select a stage to see what it does, the exact settings and where it is implemented.

      The Risk-Aware routing algorithm, as implemented

      Algorithm

      for each sensor i:  r[i] = 0.5 if HIGH_RISK else 0.0
                    tau[i] = -ln(1 - r[i])        # 0.693 or 0
tau[gateway] = 0
dist[gateway] = 0;  push (0, order=0, gateway)
while queue not empty:
    (d, _, v) = pop smallest (cost, insertion order)
    if v done: continue;  mark v done
    w = 1 + beta * tau[v]                 # beta = 10
    for u in neighbours(v)  (distance ≤ 90 m):
        if not done[u] and d + w < dist[u]:
            dist[u] = d + w;  parent[u] = v
            push (dist[u], order++, u)
install on every sensor i:  "gateway via parent[i]"

      The tree grows outward from the gateway, so w is the price of entering v on the way toward the gateway. A source never pays for its own risk, and the gateway costs 1.

      Verified assumptions

      • Oracle knowledge. Risk-Aware knows every node's true risk state. Its benefit is an upper bound.
      • Computed once, offline. Routes are fixed before traffic starts (t = 30 s) and never adapt, exactly like Static.
      • Transit-only risk. A high-risk node drops a forwarded data packet when U < 0.5, where U in [0, 1) is a SplitMix64 hash of (drop seed, node, source, sequence number), scaled as (h ≫ 11) · 2−53. Its own packets and all control, ARP and broadcast frames are never affected. The same packet gets the same decision under every protocol.
      • Same link model as Static. With no risky node, or none on Static's tree, Risk-Aware installs exactly Static's routes. This was confirmed in the uniform-placement study, where the two were identical on every metric in 7 of 12 draws.
      • Gateway never risky. Its τ is 0.

          Assumptions and limitations

          • Positions are not campus sites. Simulated sensors are uniformly random in the field. Some land on buildings, the pond or roads (counts are shown below the map).
          • No buildings in the radio model. ns-3 uses log-distance propagation (exponent 3) in open space. Walls, vegetation and terrain are not modelled, so campus links would differ.
          • Scale mismatch. The 250 m field covers about a quarter of the 0.26 km² campus. The proposed deployment spans the whole campus, so it is sparser and deeper. Under the 90 m model the default layout needs up to 7 hops with about 7 links per sensor, while every simulated topology is at most 2–3 hops deep (26–33 links per sensor at N = 100). The validated results therefore do not cover deep campus-scale meshes.
          • Traffic is a stress load. 8 kbps (and 32 kbps) of constant 512-byte UDP per sensor is far more than typical campus sensing traffic.
          • Links are a model. The 90 m disk is the routing model of Static and Risk-Aware. In ns-3, OLSR and AODV used links up to 116.6 m.

            Methodology, validation and limitations

            Method and validation

              Limitations

              • Not calibrated. No campus RSSI, packet-loss or traffic measurement exists. The radio model is open-space log-distance (exponent 3, 40 dB at 1 m), as in the validated generic study.
              • No buildings. 170 of the 284 links in the 90 m model (60 %) cross an OSM building footprint. Walls are not modelled, so link quality is likely optimistic. Footprints exist, but heights and materials do not.
              • One layout. One proposed deployment with one gateway site. Seeds vary only ns-3 randomness and the risk-drop hash, so results do not generalise to other layouts or gateway sites.
              • Placement-dependent effects. The five risk placements are not a random sample of campus risk, and one (subtree) was chosen as the worst case for Static. Most of the Risk-Aware − Static difference comes from that placement, so the averages depend on the placements chosen.
              • Stress traffic. Constant 512-byte UDP at 8 or 32 kbps per sensor is far above typical campus sensing traffic. OLSR's loss at 32 kbps (also without risk) was not investigated further.
              • Rebuilt libraries. Building the campus simulator recompiled the ns-3 libraries. Three validated configurations reproduced byte for byte with the rebuilt libraries; the rest were not re-run.
              • Same risk assumptions as the generic study: oracle risk knowledge for Risk-Aware, transit-only drops with p = 0.5, gateway never risky, flat terrain.

              5 · Conclusions, Limitations & Reproducibility

              What the evidence shows

              IEEE 802.11b study. The study identifies gateway and first-hop congestion as the dominant measurable bottleneck under near-saturated conditions and demonstrates that reducing offered traffic rate can effectively mitigate this bottleneck and improve PDR in OLSR networks at high node densities. However, the effectiveness of the intervention is protocol- and load-dependent, and a portion of packet loss remains unattributed by the current instrumentation.
              1. Scale. Increasing network scale and traffic increase congestion pressure across the three routing protocols in the Without-Risk baseline.
              2. Bottleneck. Gateway and first-hop regions are supported as major measurable bottlenecks, with OLSR gateway airtime reaching 0.86–0.89 at N≥75 under high traffic.
              3. Intervention. Traffic reduction can improve performance under overloaded conditions, with OLSR PDR improving by +14.7 percentage points at N=75 and +25.2 percentage points at N=100 at the deepest cut; Static N = 50 does not improve.
              4. V4 routing — negative result. Routing activation alone does not guarantee performance improvement; the strongest V4 activation-screen configuration re-routed 100% of seeds without a statistically credible PDR gain.
              5. Wi-Fi 6 risk-aware routing. At N = 30 (10 seeds, matched pairs), routing around high-risk relays with a risk-weighted Dijkstra raised nominal PDR over Static by +23.18 pp [95 % CI +19.81, +26.54] and cut risk exposure by 46.33 pp [95 % CI −53.21, −39.46] at k = 3, in 10/10 pairs (exact Wilcoxon p = 0.00195, Holm-adjusted 0.0117), at a p95 delay cost of +4.1 to +9.1 ms versus Static (k = 1–3; delay is over delivered packets, so Static's lower value partly reflects the relayed packets it lost). At N = 100 (3 seeds) the direction was the same in 3/3 seeds; this is reported descriptively, without a significance claim. Oracle risk knowledge makes this an upper bound.
              6. Uniform random risk placement (N = 100, 72 runs, 12 matched draws). With the high-risk sensors placed independently of every protocol, no Static − OLSR exposure difference was detected (+0.68 pp [95 % CI −1.09, +2.45] at 8 kbps; −0.45 pp [−2.18, +1.28] at 32 kbps), so the 37 pp gap of the subtree design does not appear. Risk-Aware had lower exposure and higher nominal PDR than OLSR in 12/12 draws (Holm-adjusted p = 0.006). It differed from Static only in the 5 draws with a risky node on Static's tree, where its PDR gains are descriptive and not statistically significant (section).
              7. AODV at N = 100. In this configuration AODV delivered 56.60 % against OLSR's 99.62 % (k = 0, 3 seeds); the Block Ack modification was excluded as the cause (seed 20); the mechanism is a hypothesis.

              Limitations

              IEEE 802.11b study

              • AODV: FlowMonitor PacketsSent-denominator artifact affects interpretation of PDR/PacketLoss — read as indicative, not exact.
              • OLSR: RoutingOverheadPackets = 0 is a metric-coverage limitation, not proof of zero control traffic.
              • Static: Some residual packet loss remains unattributed (64.65%–99.57% at Static/N=50, typically ≥92%).
              • Instrumentation: Not all packet-loss mechanisms are directly observable using the current counters. PHY RX drops are not directly equivalent to permanent packet loss.
              • V4: Routing adaptation did not produce measurable overall improvement, even at 100% activation.
